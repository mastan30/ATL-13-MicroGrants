Strategic Notebook Analysis & Anchor Closure Scenario

1. Available Data & Network Foundations

The notebook establishes a highly structured, scalable property graph over BigQuery by merging hard municipal data with macroeconomic models. On the factual side, it provides real business entities bounded by San Francisco's official commercial corridors, combined with real demographic profiles (ACS data), workplace job counts (LEHD data), and historical financial distress indicators (SBA 7(a) loan defaults). To connect these entities, it generates probabilistic edges: supplies relationships are derived from the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) industry purchase intensities, and draws_footfall_from edges are generated by distributing localized worker populations to nearby consumer-facing businesses. This combination effectively translates isolated spreadsheets into a traversable economic network.

2. Data Limitations & Missing Elements

The most significant limitation of the dataset is the absence of deterministic, point-to-point transaction data. The network heavily relies on modeled pairings—it asserts that a specific café probably buys from a specific local bakery based on national industry averages and geographic proximity, but it cannot definitively prove that relationship. Furthermore, the dataset lacks real-time financial health metrics (such as current rent burdens, point-of-sale revenue drops, or current cash reserves); the only hard financial distress indicator is historical SBA loan charge-offs. Geographically, the scaffolding to expand this model to other cities (like Los Angeles) is also marked as incomplete, lacking the localized neighborhood boundaries required to logically bound the network.

3. Future Work & Agentic Integration

The immediate next phase of work involves operationalizing this static graph using an AI Agent. The notebook sets up the data foundation, but an ADK-powered agent equipped with an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server is required to dynamically traverse the graph, reason about the depth of the cascade, and articulate narrative grant memos based on those findings. Future data engineering work should focus on enriching the probabilistic edges with alternative datasets—such as anonymized credit card transaction data or local mobility/cell-phone pings—to convert modeled estimates into verifiable B2B connections. Furthermore, integrating a front-end UI will be necessary to allow city officials to visually query these cascades and override the agent's assumptions.

4. Solving the "Anchor Closure" Track

The "Anchor Closure" track—where a major employer announces a departure in 90 days but no distress applicants have emerged yet—requires a proactive, predictive traversal rather than a reactive ranking. To solve this, the agent should use the employer_blocks table as the initial seed node rather than a distressed business.

The Strategy: The agent initiates a graph traversal moving downstream along the draws_footfall_from edges to instantly highlight the "first wave" of casualties—local lunch counters, dry cleaners, and retail shops that rely heavily on the anchor's workforce. It then traverses a second hop along the supplies edges to identify the "second wave" (e.g., the wholesale grocer that supplies the lunch counter).

What else to look into: To make this intervention precise, the system should incorporate commercial real estate lease expirations to accurately identify which anchor tenants are most likely to leave, and real-time foot traffic data (e.g., Placer.ai or SafeGraph) to quantify exactly how much of a local café's footfall genuinely comes from that specific office building. By quantifying the impending downstream job losses before they happen, the city can proactively approach the exposed, currently healthy businesses with transition grants before the 90-day window expires.
